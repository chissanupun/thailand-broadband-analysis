\begin{abstract}
National Internet broadband service providers, particularly in Thailand, are
often judged from user experiences rather than systematic measurement. In
Southeast Asia this assumption remains largely unstudied. For example,
Thailand ranked 12th worldwide for fixed broadband in Ookla's Speedtest
Global Index~\cite{OoklaGlobalIndex2026} (June 2026), yet whether such services reflect
application-level performance across the country is unclear. A 2023 survey
by the Thailand Consumer Council found that 81\% of 2,924 respondents had
encountered connection problems, mostly slow and dropping
connections~\cite{TCC2023}. These
numbers contradict each other for Thailand, and Thailand is not alone in
this mismatched information across the Southeast Asian region.

In this study we analyze and evaluate the communication network quality of
nine countries --- Thailand, Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, Cambodia, Indonesia,
the Philippines, Malaysia, and Singapore --- using test results from Q1 2023
to Q4 2025, drawn from open datasets from Ookla (229.4 million tests) and
M-Lab NDT7 (760.6 million tests). We compute the test-weighted share of
province-quarters that clear the bandwidth (and, for cloud gaming,
latency) requirements of four application types: voice, HD video, UHD
video and cloud gaming. Voice and HD video pass in essentially every
province-quarter, fixed and mobile, across the whole period. Fixed
broadband stratifies sharply by geography, with over a $16\times$ spread
between the strongest and weakest capital regions. At the cloud-gaming
tier, the binding constraint flips by market and connection type:
bandwidth blocks the region's lowest-tier fixed markets, while latency
blocks mobile networks in \emph{every} country in the set, including
Singapore, whose mobile network passes the bandwidth bar in 100\% of
province-quarters but the latency bar in only 48.8\%.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
National Internet Service Providers (ISPs), particularly in Southeast Asian
countries like Thailand, are frequently judged based on subjective end-user
perceptions rather than systematic empirical data. A significant gap exists
between high-level speed index rankings and actual user experiences; for
example, while Thailand was ranked 12th worldwide for fixed broadband in
Ookla's Speedtest Global Index~\cite{OoklaGlobalIndex2026} in June 2026, a 2023 survey by the Thailand
Consumer Council revealed that 81\% of 2,924 respondents suffered from
frequent connection problems such as slow speeds and dropped
signals~\cite{TCC2023}. To
address this widespread mismatch across the region, this paper presents a
comprehensive study analyzing communication network quality across nine
Southeast Asian nations: Thailand, Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, Cambodia,
Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia, and Singapore. Leveraging
cross-platform open measurement datasets collected from Q1 2023 to Q4
2025 --- comprising 229.4 million tests from Ookla and 760.6 million tests
from Measurement Lab's NDT7 --- we evaluate performance against real-world
application demands. By establishing bandwidth and latency threshold
requirements for four primary application types (voice, HD video streaming,
Ultra HD video streaming, and cloud gaming), this research provides a
multi-dimensional assessment of regional fixed broadband and cellular
networks, capturing spatial inequality, ISP performance tiers, and
application-level delivery capabilities.

\section{Related Work}
This research is performed using open datasets from Ookla and Measurement
Lab (NDT7). The next subsections cover the related work for each dataset,
followed by prior work applying the same threshold-based evaluation
methodology.

\subsection{Ookla}
\label{sec:ookla-related}
Ookla is a prominent publicly available crowdsourced performance platform
that serves as a primary foundation for empirical internet measurement.
Ookla Speedtest is one of the most widely deployed commercial frameworks.
Prior literature highlights key architectural and methodological
differences between platforms like Ookla and open research datasets such as
Measurement Lab (MLab). MacMillan et al.~\cite{MacMillan2023} show that
Ookla consistently reports higher throughput than NDT7 for the same
access line, and trace this largely to server placement: Ookla frequently
deploys measurement servers directly within access ISPs' internal
networks, whereas NDT7 uses off-net servers placed in transit networks
outside the provider's domain, and to protocol design, since Ookla opens
multiple parallel TCP streams and excludes the initial slow-start phase,
while NDT7 measures a single stream. Consequently, Ookla's approach
measures close to the maximum theoretical capacity of the immediate
broadband access network (the ``last mile''), while NDT7's single-stream,
off-net design is a closer proxy for what a single application flow, such
as web browsing or adaptive video streaming, actually experiences.
Additionally, Deng et al.~\cite{Deng2021} point out that user-initiated
tests like Ookla are subject to sampling bias and spatial multiplexing
distortions --- such as multiple devices operating behind Network Address
Translation (NAT) --- which require dedicated data curation protocols to
extract unbiased, longitudinal insights regarding ISP performance and
infrastructure bottlenecks.

The availability of open measurement data has expanded further through
Ookla's Open Data Initiative~\cite{OoklaOpenData2023}, which publicly
distributes aggregated global fixed and mobile network performance maps.
Unlike the raw, test-by-test measurements provided by MLab, Ookla's open
dataset aggregates consumer-initiated tests into standardized spatial tiles
using a Web Mercator projection (zoom level 16, approximately 600 m
$\times$ 600 m at the equator). This spatial aggregation provides
quarter-by-quarter metrics for average download speed, upload speed,
latency, tests conducted, and unique active devices. While this tiled data
masking mitigates raw IP-level privacy concerns and reduces data volume for
macro-level analysis, researchers emphasize that spatial aggregation
introduces a different trade-off. The open Ookla data inherently obscures
temporal granularities --- such as identifying specific peak-hour
congestion windows or evaluating protocol-level stack behaviors (e.g.,
individual TCP flow stats) --- which are accessible in unaggregated MLab
datasets. Consequently, empirical studies increasingly treat Ookla's open
spatial dataset and MLab's open granular dataset as complementary tools:
Ookla provides fine-grained geographic mapping of broadband availability,
whereas MLab offers deep longitudinal insight into end-to-end transport
protocol dynamics and busy-hour network degradation.

\subsection{Measurement Lab NDT7}
The Measurement Lab (MLab) Network Diagnostic Tool (NDT) protocol
represents a key benchmark in single-stream Internet performance
measurement, evolving significantly with the deployment of
NDT7~\cite{Gill2022}. Designed to measure single-stream ``bulk transport
capacity,'' NDT isolates the transport-layer throughput and round-trip time
(RTT) available to standard end-user applications. Older iterations of the
platform (web100 and NDT5) relied on TCP Reno and TCP Cubic protocols along
with non-standard ports, which often required administrative permissions or
specialized client setups. In contrast, NDT7 modernizes active measurement
by running over WebSockets over TLS via standard HTTP(S) ports (80/443).
This shift significantly expands client browser compatibility, eliminates
middlebox port-filtering artifacts, and simplifies third-party measurement
integrations without compromising network diagnostics.

A core advancement of NDT7 is its native integration of the TCP BBR
(Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time) congestion control
algorithm, which replaces loss-based algorithms like Cubic where supported.
By continuously estimating bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT, BBR
actively prevents queue buildup in intermediate buffers. In empirical
studies, NDT7 measurements show a substantial reduction in self-induced
bufferbloat, yielding lower, more accurate baseline latency metrics
compared to older single-stream tests. Researchers emphasize that NDT7
provides a much more faithful reflection of modern web traffic behavior and
realistic user application performance across interconnected broadband
networks.

\subsection{The German Internet Landscape}
Prior research by L\"ubben and Misfeld~\cite{Lubben2022} investigates the
state of Germany's Internet performance by analyzing the Measurement Lab
(MLab) open dataset using Network Diagnostic Tool (NDT) active test data
collected between January 2019 and August 2021. The authors highlight how
open broadband datasets --- despite originating from user-initiated,
uncontrolled environments --- can yield remarkably reliable insights into
end-to-end network quality and application support capabilities when
subjected to rigorous data curation. By isolating busy hours (8 p.m. to
10 p.m.), accounting for repeated measurements per IP, and controlling for
server locations and TCP congestion control protocols, the methodology
demonstrates that open data can effectively evaluate real-world application
performance. The study uses these end-to-end measurements to assess whether
current networks meet the high bandwidth and low latency demands of modern
services like HD/UHD video streaming and cloud gaming. The bandwidth and
latency requirements for different types of applications are used as a
threshold to evaluate whether services are good enough for such
applications, a methodology we adopt in Table~\ref{tab:app-requirements}.

\subsection{Regional Broadband Disparity Studies}
Two recent reports apply Ookla-based speed-test analysis to broader
regions without an application-threshold framing. Ofa and
Aparicio~\cite{Ofa2021} visualize broadband speed disparities across
Asia-Pacific economies, and Caldas et al.~\cite{Caldas2023} use OECD
speed-test data to quantify spatial disparities in internet quality within
member countries. Both studies, like most Ookla-only work, report mean or
median speeds and rankings; neither evaluates whether measured speeds
clear the bandwidth or latency bar a specific application needs, nor
combines a crowdsourced platform (Ookla) with an off-net one (NDT7) to
cross-check the result. This paper's contribution over that line of work
is exactly that combination, applied for the first time, to our knowledge,
across all nine of these Southeast Asian countries.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}
Ookla open data supplies quarterly internet-performance tiles split by
connection type (fixed, mobile) at Quadkey zoom 16
($\sim$600$\times$600 m), each carrying mean download, upload, latency, and
the underlying Speedtest submission count, delivered as global Parquet
files on Amazon S3. We map them to the nine countries and twelve quarters
(Q1 2023--Q4 2025), giving a total of 229{,}367{,}221 tests, with the
largest Indonesia test data of 74.2 million records and the smallest Laos
dataset of 875{,}799 records.

Total volume across the nine countries from MLab NDT7 is 760{,}607{,}387
tests, with the largest Indonesia test data of 367.2 million and the
smallest Laos test record of 139{,}693 records.

\section{Data Cleansing and Preparation}
The data from the two sources comes in different formats. Ookla comes as
spatial tiles approximately 600 m $\times$ 600 m, while NDT7 comes as
individual test records. We analyze the two sources \emph{separately} and
compare their results side by side (\S\ref{sec:analysis}); we do not pool
Ookla and NDT7 records into a single combined dataset, since the two
platforms measure different things (last-mile capacity versus end-to-end
single-stream throughput, \S\ref{sec:ookla-related}) and mixing them would conflate the two.
Every figure and table in this paper is drawn from one source only, named
in its caption. First, we clean the data by removing records with
throughput $\leq 0$ and duration $\leq 0$, using pandas~\cite{Pandas2020}.
We then use ip-api.com~\cite{IpApi}, a free API service, to check whether
test records carry mobile/hosting/proxy flags and to resolve the ASN for
each IP. Because ip-api assigns the mobile/hosting/proxy flag per IP
\emph{block} rather than per address, we probe 5 addresses per block
(\texttt{/24} for IPv4, \texttt{/48} for IPv6) and apply the verdict to
the whole block; across 50.5 million addresses (226,070 blocks) this
required 1.0 million probes instead of 50.5 million, completing in 11.5
hours instead of an estimated 27 days, with 44 of 226,070 blocks (0.019\%)
showing internal disagreement on manual re-check and an estimated 0.027\%
label error overall.

To compare Ookla and NDT7 at the same spatial granularity, we assign each
NDT7 test to the province it falls in using GeoPandas~\cite{GeoPandas2020}
and Shapely, performing a point-in-polygon join of client coordinates
against province/region boundaries, using \texttt{sjoin\_nearest} for
coordinates that fall outside every polygon (e.g., coastal points) to
compute distance for accuracy. NDT7 points are not re-binned into Ookla's
zoom-16 tile grid, because NDT7 client coordinates come from MaxMind
IP geolocation at city-level precision, and placing a city-level point
inside an individual $\sim$600\,m tile would imply a false precision the
underlying geolocation does not have; province-level aggregation is the
finest granularity both sources support in common.

On the Ookla dataset, we build a bounding box to define each country's
extent, referencing the geoBoundaries ADM1 file~\cite{Runfola2020} for the
nine ASEAN countries (Thailand, Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia,
Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, Singapore), then run point-in-polygon on the tiles
to determine which province each point falls in, using each country's
GeoJSON. We then compute a weighted average by each tile's test count to
prevent distortion from tiles with widely different test counts. To ensure
quality, we filter only province-quarters with \texttt{total\_tests}
$\geq 100$ and \texttt{n\_tiles} $\geq 5$, which better represents reality.
Furthermore, we found data-completeness issues in some areas. For Vietnam
and Cambodia, mobile test data is missing in some provinces for some
quarters because these are small areas with low test counts. Myanmar is
another case: a fairly large amount of data is missing on both broadband
(10.7\%) and mobile (22\%) services.

On the MLab NDT7 dataset, we query each country's test results using
BigQuery~\cite{BigQuery}, then determine the province using
point-in-polygon referencing the GADM 4.1 file~\cite{GADM2022}. We
identified and separated mobile and fixed broadband, and separated cloud
services from individual clients. Due to ISP name spelling differences,
operators are grouped using ASN rather than M-Lab's raw ISP-name field,
which is unreliable at scale. We obtain the full NDT7 test record for
every country over the studied period, including Indonesia's 367.2
million tests (\S\ref{sec:datasets}); the only stage where we subsample rather than
processing every record is the ip-api mobile/hosting/proxy lookup
described above, which classifies IP address \emph{blocks}, not
individual test records, and is therefore unaffected by which country
contributed the most tests.

For grouping operators, we rely primarily on the ASN (Autonomous System
Number), because ISP names from M-Lab can be outdated, and a single
operator can appear under hundreds of different name spellings. Using ASN
is therefore far more accurate.

\section{Data Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
Across the nine Southeast Asian countries analyzed, Ookla fixed broadband
data (2023 Q1--2025 Q4, test-weighted) reveals significant regional
performance tiers alongside pronounced urban-rural divides when comparing
capital-region performance against national averages
(Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-download}). Singapore leads the region overall, but
its Central Region (361.0 Mbps) sits \emph{below} its own national mean
(394.8 Mbps); Myanmar's Yangon (22.0) is likewise slightly below its
national mean (24.6). Every other capital region outperforms its own
country: Bangkok drives Thailand to 298.8 Mbps against a 276.9 national
mean, Kuala Lumpur lifts Malaysia to 212.3 against 194.8, Manila's NCR
reaches 159.6 against 132.0, Jakarta reaches 63.4 against 41.2, and Hanoi,
Phnom Penh and Vientiane each sit modestly above their national means.
Because these two exceptions are also the fastest and slowest capitals in
the set, the spread between the strongest and weakest capital region is
over $16\times$ (Singapore's 361.0 Mbps against Yangon's 22.0), a gap a
table of nine national averages does not surface.

\subsection{Market Structure (RQ4): The Fastest Operator Is Rarely the One People Use}
Figures~\ref{fig:fastest-operator} and \ref{fig:most-used-operator} already
show that the fastest major operator and the most-tested operator are
often not the same brand; here we quantify how often, and how large the
gap is. Pooling all twelve quarters and restricting to operators (brands,
ASNs merged) with at least 5\% test share in their country and segment,
the most-tested fixed-broadband operator is the fastest of the major
operators in only \textbf{2 of 9 countries} (Laos and Vietnam, where the
most-tested operator and the fastest happen to coincide). In the other
seven, the gap between the country's slowest and fastest major operator
ranges from Vietnam's 1.06$\times$ up to Malaysia's 4.4$\times$
(Celcom 44.1\,Mbps vs. TIME dotCom 196.3\,Mbps) and Singapore's
2.4$\times$ on broadband, 4.7$\times$ on cellular (Simba Telecom
22.2\,Mbps vs. Singtel Mobile 103.8\,Mbps) --- the largest within-market
gap we observe on either connection type. Indonesia and Myanmar show the
largest gap between what most people actually use and the fastest
available major alternative: Telkom Indonesia (the most-tested fixed
operator, 18.6\,Mbps) is 2.33$\times$ slower than Biznet (43.3\,Mbps), and
Mytel in Myanmar (10.3\,Mbps) is 2.23$\times$ slower than the fastest
major alternative (22.9\,Mbps).

We also compute each market's Herfindahl--Hirschman Index (HHI, the sum of
squared test-share percentages, a standard concentration measure) and
correlate it with the speed of that market's fastest major operator.
Concentration tracks speed on \textbf{cellular} networks (Spearman
$\rho=-0.82$, $p=0.007$, $n=9$): the more one mobile operator dominates
test share, the slower the fastest operator tends to be, consistent with
a dominant incumbent facing less competitive pressure to upgrade. On
\textbf{fixed} broadband the same relationship is essentially absent
($\rho=0.12$, $p=0.77$, $n=9$) --- fixed-line speed appears to be driven
more by infrastructure investment than by the competitive structure of the
market. As throughout this paper, we treat test share as a proxy for
market share, not a measurement of it, so this correlation is suggestive
of a market-structure effect, not proof of one.

\subsection{Peak-Hour Congestion (RQ3): Ratios, Not Absolute Latency}
Because NDT7 tests mostly terminate at offshore servers for six of our
nine countries (\S\ref{sec:analysis}), we do not treat absolute busy-hour
latency as a domestic-access measurement. Busy-versus-quiet
\emph{throughput ratios}, however, remain interpretable even under an
offshore server: the propagation delay to that server is the same in both
the busy and the quiet window, so a within-day ratio isolates congestion
on the domestic access link rather than international transit. We define each country's local busy hour as its highest-traffic clock
hour and compute the ratio of busy-hour to off-peak mean throughput.
Most countries peak in the evening (20:00--22:00), consistent with
after-work usage; Vietnam (11:00) and Cambodia (12:00) instead peak at
midday, which we read as more likely to reflect automated or bot-like
measurement schedules than genuine peak human usage, and treat with
correspondingly less confidence.

  \centering

Mobile networks degrade more at the busy hour than fixed broadband does,
and by how much varies widely by country: the busy-to-quiet cellular
throughput ratio ranges from \textbf{0.23} in Cambodia (busy-hour mobile
throughput is less than a quarter of its off-peak level) to \textbf{0.73}
in Singapore, whose mobile network shows the mildest degradation in the
set. Fixed broadband degrades less severely everywhere, though not
uniformly: Singapore's fixed broadband is the one case in our data where
busy-hour throughput \emph{exceeds} off-peak throughput
(ratio $>1$), which we read as a data artefact of very low off-peak
sample volume at that hour rather than networks that speed up under load.
Splitting by capital versus the rest of the country reverses the naive
expectation that capitals, with more capacity, should hold up better under
load: on \textbf{fixed} broadband, the capital region degrades \emph{more}
than the rest of the country in 6 of 8 countries with a capital split
(all but the Philippines and Myanmar), most sharply in Cambodia (capital
busy/off-peak ratio 0.77 versus 1.22 outside the capital --- the
non-capital ratio above 1 likely reflects thin off-peak sample volume
rather than a genuine speed-up). On \textbf{cellular}, the pattern is
close to a coin flip (capital worse in 3 of 8, rest worse in 5 of 8), with
no consistent direction. We read the fixed-broadband result as consistent
with capitals carrying denser, more contended demand at the busy hour even
though their off-peak capacity is higher (Figure~\ref{fig:capital}) ---
peak-hour behavior is a distinct phenomenon from the static capacity gap,
not a corollary of it.

\textbf{Pass rate} for an application class is the test-weighted share of
province-quarters (fixed and mobile separately, all twelve quarters,
Q1~2023--Q4~2025, computed from the Ookla exports of
Table~\ref{tab:app-requirements}'s thresholds) whose mean download speed
--- and, where a latency bound applies, mean latency --- clears the
threshold; it is an aggregate over 100+ tests per province-quarter, not a
per-test rate. For voice and HD video, whose bandwidth thresholds (64\,kbps
and 5\,Mbps) are trivially low against observed broadband speeds, the
download-side pass rate is 100\% in all nine countries, fixed and mobile,
across every quarter. Voice's 200\,ms latency bar is almost as uniform,
with one exception: Myanmar's fixed-broadband province-quarters pass it
only 98.2\% of the time (mobile: 99.1\%). Whatever the regional digital
divide in this dataset consists of, it is not a failure to carry a phone
call or a 5\,Mbps video stream.

Raising the bar to 25\,Mbps for UHD video separates the region into two
groups. Thailand, Vietnam, Singapore and Malaysia pass 100\% of
province-quarters, fixed and mobile. The Philippines is near-universal
(99.6--100\%), and Laos and Cambodia pass 88--99\%. Indonesia and Myanmar
diverge by connection type: Indonesia passes 88.1\% fixed / 90.5\% mobile,
while Myanmar passes only 22.1\% fixed but 68.8\% mobile --- the one
country in the set where mobile UHD access is substantially \emph{better}
than fixed, consistent with the mobile-first fixed--mobile gap noted
above.

Cloud gaming requires \emph{both} 44\,Mbps download and 25\,ms latency. We
evaluate latency using \textbf{Ookla}, not NDT7: for six of our nine
countries, most NDT7 tests terminate at an offshore M-Lab server (Thai
tests mainly at Singapore or Hong Kong), so NDT7 round-trip time mostly
reflects international transit rather than domestic access, whereas Ookla
selects a nearby server. Ookla latency is still an idle measurement to an
often on-net server, so it is a lower bound on what a real interactive
session would see.

Table~\ref{tab:cloudgaming} shows a binding constraint that flips by
market tier and connection type. In the three lowest-tier fixed markets
--- Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar --- bandwidth is what fails: latency clears
71--98\% of the time, but bandwidth clears only 6--67\%, capping the
combined pass rate at 0--65\%. On mobile, the pattern reverses
\emph{everywhere}: bandwidth clears 93--100\% of province-quarters in
every country except Cambodia, Laos and Myanmar, yet latency is what fails
--- including in Singapore, where mobile bandwidth clears 100\% of the
time but latency only 48.8\%, and in Thailand, Vietnam and Malaysia, where
mobile latency clears just 21.6--33.5\%. A ranking built on download speed
alone would call Singapore's and Malaysia's mobile networks cloud-gaming
ready; once latency is included, they fail roughly half the time or worse.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that basic connectivity needs
(voice, HD video) are universally met across Southeast Asia, but the
constraint that blocks the next tier of applications is not always
bandwidth. Fixed broadband stratifies sharply by geography, with over a
$16\times$ spread between the strongest and weakest capital regions
(\S\ref{sec:analysis}). At the cloud-gaming tier, low-income fixed markets
remain bandwidth-constrained, exactly as a speed-index ranking would
predict --- but mobile networks in \emph{every} country in the set,
including the region's fastest, are latency-constrained instead, a
distinction a download-speed ranking cannot see. This is, we argue, the
paper's central finding: application readiness is not one axis, and which
axis binds changes by market tier and by connection type.

\subsection{Limitations}
Both Ookla and NDT7 are crowdsourced, user-initiated platforms, not a
random sample of a country's connections: a user tests when they suspect a
problem, when they are curious, or, occasionally, when an operator wants a
favorable result, and none of these motives is uniform across countries or
operators~\cite{Deng2021}. We do not have ground truth to calibrate this
bias against, so we report distributions and pass rates rather than
treating any single figure as a population mean, and we do not interpret
test volume as subscriber count or market share (\S\ref{sec:analysis}).
Coverage is also uneven: Myanmar is missing 10.7\% of fixed and 22\% of
mobile province-quarters, and some Vietnamese and Cambodian provinces lack
mobile data in low-volume quarters; we flag these gaps rather than
interpolate across them. Finally, Ookla latency, our proxy for
cloud-gaming readiness, is an idle measurement to a nearby, often on-net
server --- a lower bound on the round-trip time a real interactive session
would see, not an upper bound, so the mobile latency failures we report in
\S\ref{sec:analysis} are, if anything, an optimistic estimate.

\section{Conclusion}
This study provides a multi-year empirical evaluation of network
infrastructure performance and application readiness across nine Southeast
Asian nations using over 990 million tests from Ookla and M-Lab NDT7
datasets. Our findings show that voice and HD-video bandwidth needs are
met in essentially every province-quarter across the region, but that
uniformity does not extend upward. Fixed broadband stratifies sharply by
geography, with capital regions reaching speeds over $16\times$ those of
the weakest capital in the set. At the cloud-gaming tier, the constraint
that actually blocks readiness is not the same everywhere: bandwidth binds
in the region's lowest-tier fixed markets (Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar), as a
speed-index ranking would predict, but latency binds mobile networks in
\emph{every} country we studied, including Singapore and Malaysia, whose
mobile networks pass the bandwidth bar almost universally yet fail the
latency bar roughly half the time or worse. This paper's central claim is
that application readiness is not a single axis reducible to a speed
ranking, and that which axis binds changes by market tier and by
connection type --- a distinction that points toward different
infrastructure investment (last-mile capacity in low-income fixed markets;
mobile-network latency engineering everywhere) than a speed ranking alone
would suggest.

\section*{Ethics and Privacy Statement}
This study relies exclusively on publicly available open datasets (Ookla
Open Data, already aggregated to spatial tiles, and M-Lab NDT7 via
BigQuery, which is public and test-level) and does not collect any new
data from individuals. NDT7 client IP addresses, already public in
M-Lab's dataset, are used only to resolve ASN, geolocation and
mobile/hosting/proxy classification via ip-api.com; they are not
published, re-identified, or used for any purpose beyond that lookup. We
do not foresee societal risks beyond those already considered by the
dataset providers' own privacy safeguards.
