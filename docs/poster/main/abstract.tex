\begin{abstract}
Southeast Asian broadband is usually judged by headline speed-index
rankings, which do not directly measure application performance. Thailand
ranked 12th worldwide for fixed broadband in July~2026~\cite{OoklaGlobalIndex2026}.
A 2023
Thailand Consumer Council online survey found 81\% of 2{,}924 mobile
subscribers reporting usage problems~\cite{TCC2023}. Across nine countries,
our corpus contains 990 million measurement records --- 229.4M from Ookla
Open Data and 760.6M from M-Lab NDT7, Q1 2023 to
Q4 2025. Using Ookla province-quarter aggregates, we compute the test-weighted
share of province-quarters whose means clear illustrative bandwidth and
latency benchmarks for voice, HD video, UHD video and cloud gaming. Both
datasets are crowdsourced and user-initiated rather than random samples. Voice and
HD video clear their bars in essentially every province-quarter across all
twelve quarters. Fixed broadband stratifies sharply by geography, with a
16$\times$ spread between the strongest and weakest capital regions. Against
the combined cloud-gaming bar, the binding constraint flips by market:
fixed broadband in Cambodia, Laos, Indonesia and Myanmar falls short mostly on
bandwidth, while five mobile networks --- including Singapore, where only
48.8\% meet the latency bound by test weight --- clear bandwidth
almost universally yet fail mostly on latency.
Work is ongoing on per-province modeling and on peak-hour behavior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
Thailand ranked 12th worldwide for fixed broadband in July~2026 in
Ookla's Speedtest Global Index~\cite{OoklaGlobalIndex2026}.
In a 2023 Thailand Consumer Council
online survey, 81\% of 2{,}924 mobile subscribers reported usage problems;
slow and dropping connections were the most common complaints~\cite{TCC2023}.
This self-selected survey does not estimate a population-wide problem rate.
We ask how nine Southeast Asian countries perform against illustrative
application benchmarks, beyond national download-speed averages.

\section{Data and Method}
We use two complementary open datasets covering Thailand, Vietnam, Laos,
Myanmar, Cambodia, Indonesia, the Philippines, Malaysia and Singapore, over
twelve quarters (Q1~2023--Q4~2025):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Ookla Open Data}~\cite{OoklaOpenData2023} --- 229{,}367{,}221
    tests, published as quarterly tiles of roughly
    600\,m$\times$600\,m, fixed and mobile. Tests often use nearby on-net
    servers and parallel streams, emphasizing access-network capacity.
  \item \textbf{M-Lab NDT7}~\cite{MLabNDT72025} --- 760{,}607{,}387
    single-stream measurement records to off-net servers, providing a
    complementary view of end-to-end throughput.
\end{itemize}

Ookla tiles are assigned to first-level administrative provinces by
point-in-polygon against geoBoundaries ADM1~\cite{Runfola2020}. NDT7
province assignments use the cleaned records' GADM 4.1 (2022) boundaries
~\cite{GADM2022}. Province averages are test-weighted. We retain Ookla
province-quarters with at least 100 tests and 5 tiles, and NDT7
province-quarters with at least 100 tests.
For NDT7, tests with non-positive throughput or duration are dropped, and IP
addresses are checked against ip-api.com to exclude hosting/proxy traffic.
Because user-initiated speed tests are a biased sample~\cite{Deng2021}, we
report aggregate threshold coverage, without inferring population-wide
application success. Coverage is uneven: Myanmar is missing 10.7\%
of fixed and 22\% of mobile province-quarters, and some Vietnamese and
Cambodian provinces lack mobile data in low-volume quarters --- gaps we
flag rather than interpolate.

We evaluate four application classes against the illustrative thresholds of
L\"ubben and Misfeld~\cite{Lubben2022}: voice (64\,kbps, 200\,ms), HD video
streaming (5\,Mbps), UHD video streaming (25\,Mbps) and cloud gaming
(44\,Mbps, 25\,ms). \textbf{Pass rate} is the test-weighted share of
province-quarters (fixed and mobile separately, all twelve quarters,
Q1~2023--Q4~2025) whose average download speed --- and, where a latency
bound applies, average latency --- clears the threshold; this is an
aggregate over 100+ tests per province-quarter. It measures the test weight
of qualifying aggregate means, not the share of users, sessions or individual
tests that meet a requirement.
We use \textbf{Ookla} latency: most NDT7 tests in six countries terminate
offshore (Thai tests mainly in Singapore or Hong Kong), mixing access and
international transit delay. Ookla instead selects a nearby server.
Its idle RTT to an often on-net server does not establish latency to an
application server under load, or actual application QoE.

\section{Preliminary Results}

\subsection{Voice and HD-video thresholds are widely met}
The voice (64\,kbps) and HD-video (5\,Mbps) bandwidth bars pass in 100\% of
province-quarters, fixed and mobile, in every one of the nine countries,
across all twelve quarters. Voice's 200\,ms latency bar is nearly as
uniform, with two exceptions: Myanmar's fixed-broadband province-quarters
have 98.2\% test-weighted coverage (mobile: 99.1\%), and Laos's mobile
province-quarters have 99.6\%. These aggregate means meet modest bandwidth
benchmarks; they do not establish call quality, streaming reliability or
the performance of individual tests.

\subsection{Fixed broadband stratifies by geography}
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../../../outputs/ookla/cross_country/03_capital_vs_national.png}
  \caption{Capital province versus national mean download speed, fixed
    broadband, test-weighted, nine countries.}
  \label{fig:capital}
\end{figure}

Most capital regions outperform their own country
(Figure~\ref{fig:capital}): Bangkok reaches 298.8\,Mbps against a national
276.9, Kuala Lumpur 212.3 against 194.8, and Manila NCR 159.6 against
132.0. Singapore and Myanmar are the exceptions: Singapore's Central
Region (361.0) sits \emph{below} its national mean (394.8), as does Yangon
(22.0 against 24.6). The spread between the strongest and weakest capital
region is over 16$\times$ (Singapore's 361.0 against Yangon's 22.0\,Mbps),
a gap that a table of national averages conceals.

Absolute speed gaps remain large. Singapore nearly
doubled its median provincial fixed speed over the period, from roughly 280
to 550\,Mbps, and Thailand grew from roughly 200 to 300. Vietnam
accelerated to join Malaysia above 200\,Mbps. Cambodia and Laos both
roughly doubled their median provincial speed over the period, reaching
61.7 and 62.2\,Mbps by Q4 2025, and Indonesia and Myanmar reached 53.8 and
53.6\,Mbps respectively --- all four remain well behind the leading tier,
but none is flat.

The fixed--mobile gap also varies widely. Fixed broadband exceeds mobile by
3.2$\times$ in Thailand and 2.1$\times$ in Singapore, but the two are near
parity in Malaysia and Indonesia (1.1$\times$), and Myanmar is the only
country in the set where mobile is \emph{faster} than fixed (0.8$\times$)
in these measurements.

\subsection{UHD video separates the region}
Raising the bar to 25\,Mbps separates the region into two groups. Thailand,
Vietnam, Singapore and Malaysia pass 100\% of province-quarters, fixed and
mobile. The Philippines is near-universal (99.6--100\%), and Laos and
Cambodia pass 88--99\%. Indonesia and Myanmar diverge by connection type:
Indonesia passes 88.1\% fixed / 90.5\% mobile, while Myanmar passes only
22.1\% fixed but 68.8\% mobile --- the one country in the set where mobile
UHD threshold coverage is substantially higher than fixed, consistent with
its measured fixed--mobile gap.

Table~\ref{tab:cloudgaming} shows a binding constraint that flips by market
tier and connection type. In four bandwidth-constrained fixed markets ---
Cambodia, Laos, Indonesia and Myanmar --- latency clears
71--98\% by test weight, but bandwidth clears only 6--67\%, capping the
combined pass rate at 0--65\%. On mobile, five countries --- Singapore,
Vietnam, Malaysia, Thailand, the Philippines --- show the pattern
reversed: bandwidth clears 93--100\% of province-quarters, yet latency is
what fails, down to just 21.6\% in Thailand. Singapore is the sharpest
case: mobile bandwidth coverage is 100\%, but combined coverage is 48.8\%.
Malaysia similarly falls from 100\% bandwidth coverage to 33.5\% combined
coverage. Download-speed comparisons miss these aggregate latency shortfalls.
The other four mobile markets have low coverage on both components:
Cambodia and Indonesia are slightly more bandwidth-constrained, while
Laos and Myanmar have latency coverage of only 2.0\% and 0.0\%, alongside
low bandwidth coverage.

\section{Ongoing and Future Work}
Three directions are in progress. First, we are fitting province-level
models of speed against economic and density covariates to examine
their associations. Second, we are extending the
NDT7 analysis to peak-hour behavior, where busy-versus-quiet \emph{ratios}
remain interpretable even though absolute latency is not, given offshore
server placement. Third, we are quantifying the measurement bias introduced
by uneven coverage and assessing sensitivity to thin-sample provinces.

Basic voice and HD-video requirements are met in almost all province-quarters.
At the cloud-gaming tier, fixed networks in Cambodia, Laos, Indonesia and
Myanmar are mainly bandwidth-limited,
while five more advanced mobile markets are latency-limited despite
sufficient bandwidth. The poster will discuss how these differences can
guide targeted infrastructure improvements.
